Nobody Wants Minutes, They Want Results

A sore neck and a walk-in massage taught me everything about how we sell our services.

CB
Cata Bonavia
24 November 2026 5 min read Customer Experience

Last Sunday my neck was stuck and my brain was on a roll.

Picture this: I woke up with a really sore neck on Sunday morning. I could only turn my neck about 40 degrees each way and not one more, which is a really fun way to start a day when friends are coming over for lunch.

After breakfast and a quick tidy of the house, I headed over to a shopping centre and entered a massage place, one of those not so fancy ones that you can walk in and still get a decent job.

“Hi, I’d like to have a massage please. My neck is really sore, I can’t move it.”

“Yes, no problem – how long would you like the massage to be? 30, 40, 50 minutes?
Only neck and shoulders? or would you also like arms and head?”

My head (the one that couldn’t turn) had exactly one thought: I don’t know, I just want this gone… do my arms, do my head, or just my neck, you tell me, because I genuinely have no idea.

I answered: “40 minutes” Middle ground, very brave, I know.

And if you’ve ever had a chat with me you already know what came next. I sat in that chair redesigning their customer experience while a stranger worked on my neck. (Occupational Hazard that never goes off, no matter how bad my neck is).

The massage was wonderful, the masseuse that was treating me suggested I did 10 more minutes if I could stay.

My answer? “Make it 20 if you need, but please help me.”

And so she did.

Somewhere between my right arm and left shoulder she helped me answer the question I’d been playing in my head (imaginary post it included).

Can you keep a secret? Turns out nobody wants minutes, they want results

I didn’t want a 40 minute massage, all I wanted was to turn around my head again with no pain.

The front counter asked me to pick a product.
The masseuse asked what was wrong and then used that, her hands and what she knows to recommend what would help me the most.

Same shop, same Sunday, two completely different experiences.

Let’s dig in:

The first approach makes the customer:

  • Learn your service menu before they can get any help.
  • Handle the complexity and decision making.
  • Second guess their knowledge about their problem.

The second approach:

  • Uses your expertise to do the mental work for them.
  • It helps them trust you and your knowledge more.
  • It positions you as the one who has their sh* together without making them doubt about their own capabilities.

Once you start looking it’s everywhere (not me trying to get you my own occupational hazard lol)

The person who books a 30 minute meeting with their accountant when what they really want is to stop lying awake at night about their numbers. (And probably still do because the accountant didn’t really speak in English, they spoke accountanty – but that’s for another issue).

The family in an intake meeting being asked what support package they want when all they want is for their kid to improve their behaviour or health.

Or the organisation that books a customer experience workshop with me.
Nobody wakes up wanting three workshops, they want fewer things falling through the cracks, less friction for their teams, lower costs and higher impact. Workshops might be the way I help them get there, but they are a tool, just like massaging with oil or without oil, or on the arm or the head.

Still this SHiFT: Ask what they came for before you ask if they want to upgrade their combo for 50 cents.

To be fair this is not something that happens just to a few or that it happens because you don’t care.

It happens because when you’re buried in rosters, budgets and a to-do list that grows overnight redesigning your offers or improving customer interactions feels a lot harder than just selling minutes and counting workshops. None of this is anyone’s fault, it’s just how it’s been done for years.

But… if we just bill minutes and ask about hours we can’t differentiate from the massage place next to us. It’s just a pricing game.

Take this to your next team meeting:

What are people actually trying to achieve when they come to us? If in 12 months (or 45 minutes) we are popping a bottle of champagne with them what are they celebrating?

Is it that we delivered 4 workshops and 3 surveys or is it that we’ve increased customer loyalty and created a niche of their own where they can thrive in a crowded market?

Is it that the kid had 10 sessions of speech pathology or is it that they can now roll their Rs?

Your move: When was the last time you did a guestimate?
When were you last asked to choose something as a customer with no idea what you actually needed?
Or on the flip side…
when are you asking your customers to choose based on how your team works instead of what they came here to achieve?

I’m collecting examples, because I’m a bit of a nerd and (honestly) because I’d love to use them for other issues (with permission).

And yes, my neck is feeling a lot better and I’ll be back to see May, who is 78 years and stronger than many of us together, very soon.

Purpose is a promise. CX is how you keep it.

Cata
