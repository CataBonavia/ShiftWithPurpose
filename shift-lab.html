The opportunity

You already have more value than you're capturing.

The training you built for your own staff, that three other organisations have already asked to borrow. The assessment tool you've refined over eight years. The methodology you explain for free on every panel you sit on.

The relationships, expertise and ways of working you've built because you've spent years solving problems other organisations don't know how to solve.

None of that is a new business. It may be the business you already have, sold to someone who is not the person you currently serve.

An organisation whose entire direction can be redrawn by one unsuccessful funding round isn't running a strategy, it's renting one. What you have is a landlord disguised as a funder.

Funders matter and they should be part of your strategy. They shouldn't be able to switch the whole thing off.

What this is

Find what's hiding in plain sight. Build what's next.

This isn't about finding another funding source. It's about creating something people genuinely want to buy, using capabilities and knowledge your organisation already has.

How it works

Six phases. One offer. Real buyers at the end.

Weeks 1–3

See

We understand the organisation as it actually is today. Your current model, customers, capabilities, assets, unmet needs, constraints and readiness. We find what's already working, what's getting in the way, and where there might be something worth building.

You leave with: a sharp diagnosis of what's holding you back from your next chapter. Not sixty pages that end up in a drawer.
Weeks 4–6

Hunt

We look for the value hiding in plain sight. What do you know? What do you have? What do people need? Where could value be created? And, crucially, who would pay for it? We develop three possible futures for the organisation, not three variations of the same safe idea.

You leave with: your Opportunity Map and the Three Futures.
Weeks 7–8

Back it

Now we make the decision. Each opportunity is tested against impact, demand, income, capability and feasibility. One gets backed; not because it's the most exciting idea in the room, but because it has the strongest case for investment.

You leave with: one prioritised opportunity and the reasoning a board can follow. This is often the moment you see something you couldn't see before.
Weeks 9–12

Build

Now we make it real. The offer, the customer, the value proposition, the delivery model, the pricing, the capability required, and the assets that make it sellable and deliverable.

You leave with: the offer, built, plus the business case for taking it forward.
Weeks 13–14

Test

We put the smallest useful version of the offer in front of real buyers. A paid pilot, a pre-sale, a prototype, a customer commitment, or another test designed to generate the strongest possible evidence.

You leave with: real market evidence, not opinions about whether the idea is good.
Weeks 15–16

Take it forward

The final two weeks turn evidence into a decision and a practical next move. Go, refine or kill. And a 90-day roadmap built around the reality of your team, your systems and your constraints.

You leave with: a clear path to putting your new offer into your customers' hands. No wishful thinking, and no "now someone needs to implement the strategy".
The Three Futures

Three real options, chosen deliberately, instead of falling in love with the first idea in the room.

Extend
What could we sell differently?

Take something you already do and offer it to a new audience, or in a new way.

Productise
What could we package and repeat?

Turn existing expertise, methodology or IP into something repeatable and scalable.

Reinvent
What could we create that doesn't exist today?

Build something genuinely new from an emerging or unmet need.

Will it strengthen the mission?

Demand

Income

Feasibility

The questions I always get

Asked and answered.

Isn't this just commercialising our mission?

The value already exists, and you're currently giving much of it away. I'm not asking you to sell your purpose, or to charge the people you serve. I'm asking a different question: who else has a problem your organisation is uniquely equipped to solve, and would pay you to solve it?

Our people can't pay for anything.

Correct. The buyer for the new offer usually isn't the person you serve today. That's the point.

Won't this pull the team away from mission work?

It will, if you bolt another business onto the organisation. It shouldn't, if the offer is designed properly. The delivery model and pricing need to cover the real cost of delivering it before you scale. That's part of the work we do together.

We tried a social enterprise before and it didn't work.

Often the problem is that the social enterprise becomes a second business built around capabilities the organisation doesn't actually have. We start somewhere else: what are you already exceptionally good at, and who else needs it?

We've tried consultants before and nothing stuck.

The most common thing I hear. Most consulting produces reports. You leave here with equipment, and with evidence about whether anyone will pay.

What if the idea doesn't work?

Then I'll tell you, and that's why we test before you invest heavily in building it. Before we get anywhere near a kill decision, we'll adapt and iterate to find a version that does work. You may leave with a Go, a Refine or a Kill, and all three are useful decisions.
