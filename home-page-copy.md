# 2. THE OPPORTUNITY

## Your next source of income is already inside your organisation.

The training you built for your own staff, that three other organisations have already asked to borrow. The assessment tool you have refined over eight years. The methodology your team explains for free on every panel they sit on. The service a corporate would happily pay for, that you currently offer as volunteering.

The relationships, the expertise, the data and the trust you have built because you spent years solving problems other organisations put in the too-hard basket.

**None of that is a new business. It may be the business you already have, sold to someone who is not the person you currently serve.**

For thirty years the sustainability conversation in this sector has had two settings: apply for more, or run an event. There is a third one, and almost nobody names it.

> **First came grants. Then came diversification. Now comes income you own.**

Grants built this sector and they pay for work that should never be sold. The sector needs more of them, not fewer. What it needs alongside them is something nobody else gets to switch off, because an organisation whose entire direction can be redrawn by one unsuccessful funding round is not running a strategy, it is renting one.

# 3. THE HARD PART

## Doing good doesn't earn you a pass on a half-baked service.

It used to. Show up with a good cause and people forgave the intake form that asked for funding details before it asked someone's name, the process nobody could explain, the experience that depended entirely on who happened to answer the phone.

It isn't enough now. Your clients are smart and they have alternatives. Your funders want to invest in something genuinely worth it. Your staff get to choose what they stand for and what they want to experience every day.

Here is the part nobody says out loud. **Grant funding hides all of it**, because the person paying is not the person experiencing it. An acquittal never asks whether the intake felt human. So an organisation can be mediocre for years without consequence, and most of the people inside it half know.

The moment someone pays you directly, quality stops being a values question and becomes a revenue question. You cannot sell something you are only just getting away with.

Which is good news, in a way. Building something people will pay for is the fastest way to find out how good your service actually is.

# 4. WHAT YOU END UP WITH

## You'll end up with something real, not a set of recommendations.

Fourteen years of this work, across hundreds of organisations, and the pattern holds: the answer is rarely a report. Usually it's a thing.

**A housing project in Buenos Aires** needed bathrooms for families who could not afford a plumber. What they got was a design buildable in eight hours, by someone with no prior knowledge and no specialist tools.

**An organisation supporting people with intellectual disabilities** needed career conversations that actually worked. What they got was a set of cards that outperformed every formal process they had tried, and cost less than a hundred dollars to make.

**A large Victorian university** needed a student placement experience that teachers didn't dread. What they got was the whole thing redesigned end to end, with the people who deliver it.

**Organisations sitting on years of expertise** needed a way to turn it into income. What they got was a new offer, costed, priced and tested with real buyers before anyone committed to building it.

Different sectors, different formats, one method. Find what's already there, design something people will actually use, and stay until it works.

**Never a report for the drawer.**
